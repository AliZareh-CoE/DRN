RESPONSE TO REVIEWER
Question: "Why didn't the authors compare against AutoML approaches?"
======================================================================

We thank the reviewer for this valuable suggestion. To address this concern
rigorously, we benchmarked DRN against five classical machine learning methods
(Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, SVM with RBF kernel, and
MLP) and two leading AutoML frameworks (H2O AutoML and AutoGluon) on the
identical 4-class odor discrimination task using the same train/test split
(n_train = 360, n_test = 200, d = 1,344 features). H2O AutoML and AutoGluon
were selected as they are widely regarded as the top-performing open-source
AutoML frameworks for tabular data, consistently ranking highest in published
benchmarks (Erickson et al., 2020; Gijsbers et al., 2024). Both were given
a 5-minute training budget to enable full pipeline search and ensemble
construction. The results are summarized in the new Table \ref{tab:complexity}
and Figure \ref{fig:complexity}, and reveal three key findings:

First, DRN achieves 86.5% test accuracy, substantially outperforming the
best AutoML framework (AutoGluon, 67.5%; +19.0 percentage points) and the
best classical method (MLP, 65.5%; +21.0 pp). This performance gap reflects
a fundamental limitation of AutoML approaches for neural data: they require
flattening the 32 x 21 (channel x frequency band) matrices from each brain
region into a single 1,344-dimensional vector, destroying the spatial-spectral
structure that DRN's convolutional architecture preserves natively. Furthermore,
AutoML treats the concatenated OB and PCx features as an undifferentiated
input, whereas DRN's dual-stream architecture with attention-based fusion
learns to dynamically weight region-specific contributions---a design that
mirrors the known functional hierarchy of the olfactory circuit.

Second, DRN's inference complexity is O(P), meaning per-sample latency
(25.96 ms on CPU) remains constant regardless of training set size. This is
a critical requirement for real-time brain-machine interface deployment. By
contrast, SVM inference scales as O(n_sv x d) and would degrade approximately
44-fold at n = 50,000 training samples, while DRN's latency would remain
unchanged (see asymptotic analysis in Figure \ref{fig:complexity}b--e).

Third, DRN provides neuroscientifically interpretable outputs through its
channel and spatial attention mechanisms, revealing which electrode contacts
and frequency bands drive classification. AutoML ensembles, by nature, offer
limited transparency into the neural coding principles underlying odor
discrimination.

We acknowledge that DRN requires longer training time (2,520 s vs. ~300 s for
AutoML), which is expected given the 5-fold cross-validated deep learning
pipeline. However, training is a one-time cost, and the accuracy advantage
of +19.0 pp over AutoGluon---alongside constant-time inference and
interpretability---justifies this investment for the neuroscience application
at hand.
